Seabird monitoring and conservation

Following threatened seabirds at sea and at their colonies, from the Mediterranean to the Atlantic and Pacific.

Status: Scopoli’s shearwater, Linosa, 2010–2020, ongoing collaboration; Bermuda petrel project 2023–2024; Galápagos petrel fieldwork 2024

Scopoli’s shearwaters on Linosa. From my undergraduate years into my PhD I worked on an EU LIFE project on Linosa, monitoring the Scopoli’s shearwater (Calonectris diomedea) colony while controlling introduced rats and mice. In 2020 I began monitoring the colony with passive acoustic recorders and radar, with the support of Ornis italica. The same colony gave two field experiments: on how artificial light and sound from tourism affect chick weight, and on whether parents recognise their own chick (they adopt foster chicks unconditionally, so probably not).

Bermuda petrel. With colleagues in Portugal, Canada and Bermuda I worked on acoustic monitoring and GPS tracking of the Bermuda petrel or cahow (Pterodroma cahow), thought extinct since the 17th century and rediscovered in the 1950s. I co-directed, with Letizia Campioni, a 2023–2024 grant from Environment and Climate Change Canada to help increase its population. Tracking showed the species forages mainly at the sea surface and flies more at night.

White-tailed tropicbird. Also on Bermuda, citizen-science records of first sightings from 1953 to 2023 point to possible multi-decadal changes in when white-tailed tropicbirds (Phaethon lepturus) arrive at their breeding grounds.

Galápagos petrel. In 2024, with the Jocotoco Conservation Foundation, I carried out fieldwork and GPS tagging of the Critically Endangered Galápagos petrel (Pterodroma phaeopygia) at its largest colony, on Floreana Island, to study the species’ foraging ecology.

Methods: passive acoustic monitoring, marine radar, GPS tracking, citizen-science data.
