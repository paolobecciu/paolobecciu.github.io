Flight and migration

How do flying migrants handle wind, uplift and open sea? Radar and GPS tracking of soaring birds across the Mediterranean and worldwide.

Status: PhD work 2015–2020 at the University of Haifa, with ongoing collaborations

My interest in flight started with watching shearwaters dynamic-soar over the sea, trading speed for altitude in the wind. It grew into a PhD at the Animal Flight Lab, University of Haifa, on how soaring migrants such as storks and raptors harvest energy from thermal uplift and tailwinds, and how they cope where that energy runs out.

Water bodies are a hard case: thermals barely form over the sea, so a crossing is costly and risky. I studied how birds integrate wind conditions and geography to decide when to cross, how fast to fly and where to route around, using marine radar and GPS-accelerometer tags on species migrating between Africa and Europe.

Part of this work was developed during a nine-month stay (2019–2020, supported by an Erasmus+ fellowship) in Kamran Safi’s group at the Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior in Konstanz, as a global analysis of soaring birds crossing geographical barriers.

Key findings

  • Movement data from 50 bird and bat species flying in the wild revised the classic ranking of locomotion costs, suggesting that flying can be cheaper than swimming though almost as costly as running, depending on the physical environment, morphology and behaviour (Scacco et al. 2026, Science).
  • Juvenile black kites crossed the Strait of Gibraltar in stronger crosswinds and at wider sections than adults during easterly winds, and drifted extensively where adults compensated by flapping; this suboptimal behaviour may lower juvenile survival (Santos et al. 2024, Proceedings of the Royal Society B).
  • Radar-tracked soaring migrants in central Israel adjusted to the daily sea-breeze cycle: they lowered their airspeed as tailwinds grew, keeping ground speed at about 16–17 m/s, and fully compensated for lateral drift when crosswinds peaked (Becciu et al. 2023, Movement Ecology).
  • Across all major flyways, facultative soaring birds crossing the open sea chose routes that maximised wind support and, when wind support was suitable, greater uplift, suggesting that uplift as well as wind shapes the cost of sea crossings (Nourani et al. 2021, Proceedings of the Royal Society B).
  • In fog, soaring migrants at the Strait of Messina scattered more widely in flight direction and drifted with crosswinds: the first quantitative evidence of how fog changes bird flight behaviour (Becciu et al. 2021, Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution).
  • Tailwinds led migrating white storks to cross Iskenderun Bay in southern Turkey rather than detour over land; in spring, birds that crossed travelled 60 km further per day, reaching their destination faster but likely at a higher energy cost (Becciu et al. 2020, Functional Ecology).
  • A review of radar studies of migrating insects and birds found an overlooked similarity in how very small (e.g. aphids) and very large (e.g. vultures) migrants use thermal updrafts, and proposed that many aerial migrants adjust their flights to the interplay between weather and geography (Becciu et al. 2019, Ecography).
  • Raptors migrating past the Strait of Messina flew faster over the ground and lowered their airspeed with tailwinds, but fewer passed when tailwinds prevailed, suggesting that tailwinds led them to cross the sea rather than detour over land; they compensated for winds blowing towards the sea but drifted with winds blowing towards land (Becciu et al. 2018, Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology).

Methods: marine radar, GPS-accelerometer tracking, atmospheric data annotation, circular statistics, generalised additive models.
